# Phishing detection essentials

In this course you will learn about the different types of Phishing.

- Fake Password Resets
- Delivery Scams
- Impersonated Services
- Credential Harvesting 
- Malware
- Invoice Frauds
- Business email Compromise
- Fake Shared documents

## Definitions of the phishing types
### Fake Password Resets 
These attacks send urgent notifications claiming your account has been compromised or requires an immediate password update. The message includes a link to a fraudulent login page designed to capture your existing credentials when you try to change them.
### Delivery Scams 
Exploiting online shopping habits, these emails or texts pretend to be major courier services reporting a missed delivery or held package. They urge you to click a link to reschedule, pay a small fake fee, or verify your address, ultimately stealing payment info or personal data.
### Impersonated Services 
Scammers clone the branding and tone of trusted everyday tools to trick you into taking action. Because the message looks official, victims trust the prompt to "update billing details" or "verify identity" without second-guessing its authenticity.
### Credential Harvesting 
This tactic specifically targets your usernames, passwords, and security tokens by directing you to realistic fake landing pages. Once you enter your credentials, the attacker captures them in real-time, often gaining unauthorized access to your accounts before you realize what happened.
### Malware 
These phishing attempts use enticing or alarming messages to trick you into downloading malicious attachments or clicking links that secretly run harmful software. Once installed, malware can keylog your typing, steal sensitive files, or grant hackers full remote control over your device.
### Invoice Frauds 
Attackers send fake bills or altered billing statements that look like legitimate requests from vendors, suppliers, or utilities. They rely on urgency and professional formatting so accounting teams or individuals pay the balance directly into an attacker-controlled bank account.
### Business Email Compromise (BEC) 
In a BEC attack, cybercriminals compromise or spoof a high-level executive’s or trusted partner’s email address. They then send targeted requests to employees—often demanding urgent wire transfers, gift card purchases, or sensitive tax data under the guise of an emergency confidential project.
### Fake Shared Documents 
These messages notify you that a colleague or familiar service has shared a file with you via platforms like Google Drive, OneDrive, or Dropbox. Clicking the access link takes you to a fake login portal or prompts you to enable macros, leading directly to credential theft or malware installation.

## How to protect yourself against them 
### Fake Password Resets 
Look out for unexpected requests when you haven't recently asked to reset an account. Check the sender's actual email address for typos, and hover over links to ensure the destination URL matches the official website exactly rather than a slightly misspelled domain.
### Delivery Scams 
Be suspicious of unexpected tracking updates, especially those demanding immediate payment to release a package. Legitimate delivery services rarely ask for personal details via random SMS or email, and they typically direct you to track shipments through their official app or verified site.
### Impersonated Services 
Watch for subtle anomalies in the sender's domain (e.g., @support-netflix.com instead of @netflix.com). Greeting lines like "Dear Customer" instead of your actual name, combined with high-urgency demands to fix billing issues immediately, are major warning signs.
### Credential Harvesting 
Always inspect the browser’s address bar before typing login details; fake pages often use URLs with minor misspellings or unusual extensions. Additionally, be wary of pages that lack standard security indicators, load strangely, or do not support password manager auto-fill features.
### Malware 
Be cautious of emails pushing you to download unexpected file attachments, especially compressed archives (.zip), executable files (.exe), or Microsoft Office documents asking to "Enable Macros." Vague messages claiming an attached file is urgent or confidential should raise immediate flags.
### Invoice Frauds 
Watch for unexpected updates to a vendor's bank account details or sudden requests for quick payment on an unfamiliar purchase order. Always verify billing changes using a separate, trusted communication channel—like calling the supplier at a known, official phone number.
### Business Email Compromise (BEC) 
Be on high alert if an executive or authority figure sends an unusually urgent email requesting confidential wire transfers, gift cards, or employee data while claiming they are unavailable to talk on the phone. Check the full email address carefully to ensure it isn't spoofed.
### Fake Shared Documents 
Watch for notifications asking you to log in to an external site just to view a simple document. Real cloud storage services allow you to preview files directly or access them seamlessly if you are already signed in to your account.

## Indicators that give away the scam and their common names
### Generic Greeting 
Look for impersonal, vague salutations like "Dear Customer," "Valued Member," or "Dear User" instead of your actual name. Legitimate organizations with which you have an account typically address you personally or by your username. 
### Urgency 
Watch for aggressive language demanding immediate action, such as "Account suspended within 24 hours," "Immediate action required," or "Legal action pending." Attackers use artificial time pressure to induce panic so you act before thinking critically.
### Suspicious Links 
Check destination addresses by hovering your mouse over hyperlinks before clicking. Red flags include domain names with subtle typos, extra characters (e.g., paypal-security-update.com instead of paypal.com), unexpected domain extensions, or URLs shortened through third-party services.
### Credential Requests 
Be wary of any unexpected message asking you to enter or verify sensitive information like passwords, PINs, or full credit card details. Reputable organizations rarely, if ever, ask you to verify credentials via an unprompted email or message link.
### Attachment 
Exercise caution with unexpected file attachments, particularly compressed archives (.zip, .rar), script files, or Office documents prompting you to enable macros. Phishing messages often use enticing or frightening filenames like Invoice_Overdue.pdf.exe to trick you into opening them.
### Spelling Errors 
Notice frequent grammatical mistakes, strange sentence phrasing, improper capitalization, or awkward translations. While major companies occasionally make typos, a high density of spelling and grammatical errors is a key indicator of a low-quality phishing campaign.
### Unusual Requests 
Identify demands that deviate significantly from standard operational procedures, such as an executive asking for gift card purchases, a vendor asking to send payment to a new offshore bank account, or a service requesting administrative access.
### Impersonation 
Examine the exact display name and full email address of the sender. Attackers often alter display names to mimic executives or trusted brands while sending from unrelated or slightly misspelled email addresses (e.g., support@m1crosoft.com).
